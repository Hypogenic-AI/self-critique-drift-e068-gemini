\section*{Abstract}
Large Language Models (LLMs) are frequently prompted to self-reflect and revise their answers, often leading to performance gains. However, it remains unclear whether these gains correspond to a fundamental reshaping of internal representations or merely shallow re-sampling. We investigate this by measuring \textit{representation drift} in the residual stream during self-critique across different model scales and reasoning tasks (\gsm and \csqa). Our results show that while representation drift occurs in all reflection conditions, autonomous self-critique in a \qwenonefive model fails to improve the linear separability of correct reasoning, yielding results similar to a simple rewrite control. In contrast, providing an \external critique significantly restructures the latent space, increasing linear probe AUC from $0.486$ to $0.831$. Interestingly, the smaller \qwenzerofive model shows successful autonomous restructuring (AUC $0.550$ to $0.719$), suggesting that the relationship between reflection, scale, and internal state is non-trivial. Our findings imply that meaningful self-reflection requires specific structural shifts in the reasoning manifold, which autonomous self-critique may not consistently trigger in mid-sized models without external guidance.
